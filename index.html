I Broke My RAG Pipeline Five Times. Here's What I Actually Learned.

Building a RAG system looks simple on paper — five steps, clean diagram, done. Then you actually build one. Every fix breaks something else. That's not a bug in your process. That's the job.

read full note ▾

The architecture of a RAG system fits on a napkin. Parse a document. Chunk the text. Embed the chunks. Store them in a vector database. At query time, embed the question, retrieve the closest chunks by cosine similarity, pass them as context to an LLM, get an answer.

Five steps. Clean diagram. Looks obvious.

I built one. It took me weeks. And almost every day of those weeks, something that was working stopped working because I fixed something else. This is the story of that, and what it taught me about what AI engineering actually means.

Break #1 — Chunking by word count

My first version split text every 500 words with a 50-word overlap. Simple, works in tutorials. In practice, it splits mid-sentence constantly. A chunk would end with "The patient was prescribed" and the next chunk would start with "metformin 500mg twice daily". Neither chunk is semantically complete on its own.

When you embed an incomplete thought, the vector doesn't represent the meaning — it represents noise. The retrieval step returns chunks that score high on cosine similarity but contain half an idea. The LLM then tries to answer from half-ideas and either hallucinates the missing half or says it doesn't know.

I spent two days debugging what I thought was a retrieval problem. Changed the number of results returned. Changed the similarity threshold. Nothing helped. Eventually I printed the raw chunks and read them. That's when I saw it — the chunks themselves were broken.

Lesson: garbage in, garbage out. An LLM cannot reason correctly from semantically broken input, no matter how good the model is.

Break #2 — Switching embedding models

After fixing chunking, retrieval improved — but it was slow. My embedding model (nomic-embed-text via Ollama) was running locally and taking 3-4 seconds per chunk on a large document. I switched to a smaller, faster model.

Retrieval immediately got worse. Questions that previously returned the right chunk started returning tangentially related ones. Why? Because different embedding models have different vector spaces. nomic-embed-text is trained on a large diverse corpus and produces 768-dimensional vectors with strong semantic structure. The smaller model I switched to produces 384-dimensional vectors — faster to compute, but with less nuanced similarity distinctions.

The cosine similarity scores were still high — the numbers looked fine. But "high similarity in a weaker vector space" is not the same as "semantically relevant." The metric was lying to me because the space it was measuring in had changed.

Lesson: when you change an embedding model, you're not just changing speed — you're changing what "similar" means. You have to re-evaluate retrieval quality from scratch, not just check that the numbers look similar.

Break #3 — Retrieving more chunks to improve context

My retrieval was returning top-2 chunks. Some answers felt incomplete, so I bumped it to top-5. The logic made sense: more context, better answers.

The opposite happened. Answers got worse. The LLM started contradicting itself within a single response. It would say "the document states X" in one sentence and "the document states not-X" two sentences later.

The problem: chunks 3, 4, and 5 weren't the second-most-relevant content — they were the content that happened to use similar words but came from a completely different context in the document. When you pack five chunks into a context window, especially from different sections, the LLM doesn't know which one to trust. It tries to synthesize all of them into a coherent answer and produces incoherent output.

More context is not always better context. Relevant and focused beats large and noisy.

Lesson: retrieval quality matters more than retrieval quantity. Two highly relevant chunks outperform five mediocre ones every time.

Break #4 — The system prompt I stopped thinking about

At some point my answers started getting verbose. The LLM would give a correct answer, then keep going — adding context it wasn't asked for, speculating beyond the document, sometimes contradicting the correct answer it just gave.

I checked chunking. I checked retrieval. Both were fine. The issue was the system prompt I had written weeks earlier and stopped thinking about: "You are a helpful assistant. Answer the user's question."

"Helpful" to an LLM means thorough. It means adding context. It means not leaving the user with an incomplete picture. That instruction was actively fighting against what I wanted — a precise, document-grounded answer. The model was following its training, not my intent, because I hadn't been specific enough about my intent.

I rewrote it: "Answer using ONLY the provided context. If the answer is not in the context, say so. Be concise." Three constraints. The verbosity stopped immediately.

Lesson: the system prompt is part of the pipeline. It's not configuration — it's code. It needs the same precision you'd give a function signature.

What this actually taught me about AI engineering

Every one of these breaks had the same pattern: I changed one thing to fix a problem and introduced a different problem elsewhere. The pipeline was a system — each component's output was another component's input — and I wasn't thinking about it as a system. I was thinking about it as five independent steps.

That shift in thinking — from "fix the broken part" to "understand how the parts interact" — is the difference between someone who can get a demo working and someone who can build something reliable.

AI engineering right now gets conflated with prompt engineering, with vibe coding, with describing what you want to an agent and watching it appear. That approach gets you a first version. It doesn't get you a system that works on documents it has never seen, at 2am, for a user you've never met.

Reliability comes from understanding every component well enough to predict how it fails. Scalability comes from knowing which assumptions hold at 10 documents and which ones collapse at 10,000. Debuggability comes from building so that when something breaks — and it will — you can isolate exactly where.

None of that comes from prompting. It comes from building, breaking, and understanding why it broke.

The diagram is five boxes and four arrows. The engineering is everything that happens when reality doesn't match the diagram.
